If someone had told my childhood dance teacher that the little girl standing quietly in the corner of her class would one day travel across cities, countries and life changes to continue learning Kathak, I wonder what she would have said!

Actually, she might have just smiled. She still remembers that little girl rather well — especially her stubbornness and, apparently, her puffy cheeks! 😄

## The girl who wouldn't dance

It all began when my mother enrolled me in a nearby classical dance class. My nursery school teacher had noticed that I seemed interested in dancing and perhaps had a natural inclination towards it. A few children from our neighbourhood were attending the same class, so my mother thought it would be a good hobby for me.

There was just one little problem. 😂

I didn't want to dance.

I was a rather stubborn child. I would happily go to class, but once there, I would quietly stand in a corner with my arms folded and my cheeks puffed up. I refused to join the other children. My teacher, Dr. Neha Shukla — whom we all fondly called Neha Didi — still remembers this phase of mine.

This continued for months. Looking back, I wish I could ask my younger self what exactly she was protesting against. But honestly, I have absolutely no idea!

Then, one day, when most of the students were absent, something changed. Perhaps seeing fewer people around made me feel a little less conscious. I decided to give it a try and started imitating the movements I had watched the other children perform.

Neha Didi was delighted. She appreciated my attempt and asked me, "You did well today! Why don't you dance every day? Will you dance in the next class?"

I don't remember what I said, but apparently, I just smiled.

And I did return to dancing in the next class.

Gradually, I started enjoying it so much that I would sometimes refuse to go home after class. I wanted to stay back, dance a little more and watch the senior students practise. Somewhere along the way, the little girl who wouldn't dance had started looking forward to it.

## The Board Exam Fever

A few years passed, and then came the Class 10 board examinations. Like some of the other children in my neighbourhood, I stopped attending dance classes after Class 10. By then, I had completed most of the Kathak examinations I had planned to take, and I thought that perhaps this was where my dance journey would pause.

Studies gradually took over. I chose science in Classes 11 and 12, moved to another city for graduation and eventually joined the NCC. My priorities had changed, and Kathak took a back seat.

But it never disappeared completely.

Even when I wasn't practising, the bols would occasionally come back to me. Reciting them still felt natural, almost as if my memory had quietly kept them safe. I also participated in a few college events, but regular practice was no longer part of my routine.

At that point, I didn't really know when or whether I would return to Kathak.

## A bicycle, a new city and a restart 🚲

After graduation, I moved to another city for my master's degree. During my final year, I found myself surrounded by friends who were practising Bharatanatyam and vocal music. They started looking for teachers and made efforts to reconnect with their respective art forms.

Watching them inspired me. If they could find their way back to their art, perhaps I could too.

Of course, I had plenty of other plans. I wanted to do something extraordinary with my master's thesis and had big research ambitions. I also had a dream of joining the defence forces. Yet, somewhere between all these plans, I couldn't stop thinking about dance.

After a fair bit of searching and logistical struggle, I found Roshan Tai, who taught near my apartment. I even borrowed a bicycle from a classmate to get to her classes. And just like that, I was back!

I was around 22–23 years old and felt a little like an adult beginner. Many of the students were younger than me, but they were already senior and advanced learners. Their level of practice and understanding sometimes made me question how much I had forgotten.

The style was also different from what I had learnt earlier. It made me realise that there was so much more to Kathak than I had experienced as a child. There were different ways of approaching the same art form, and so much more to explore.

I was happy to be dancing again, even if I occasionally felt a little lost.

Unfortunately, this restart lasted only a few months. I had to move to another city for an internship. I couldn't find a teacher there, and the internship itself kept me quite busy.

But the love for art continued to find its way into our everyday lives. My roommates were the same Bharatanatyam dancer and musician friends who had inspired me to return to Kathak. We would often collaborate, choreograph little pieces and perform simply because we enjoyed creating something together.

Our audience was usually our landlords — a very sweet and encouraging family who became our only cheerleaders! 😄

Those little performances may not have been grand productions, but they remain some of my fondest memories. They reminded me that art doesn't always need a stage or an audience. Sometimes, all it needs is a few people who enjoy creating together.

## Finding people who cared about my journey

After my internship, I spent some time with my family and then moved again, this time for a job. I thought I would be staying there for a longer period, so I started looking for a Kathak teacher.

I met Bhagyalakshmi Tai, who introduced me to Lalita Kaku. They became an important part of my journey during the next couple of years.

Lalita Kaku, in particular, taught with immense dedication. She encouraged me, pushed me and sometimes scolded me when I wasn't putting in the effort she expected. And I genuinely appreciated it. It felt good to have someone who cared about my learning and wanted me to do better.

Yet, restarting Kathak wasn't always easy. I often felt that I wasn't progressing as much as I should. I was also struggling with the feeling that I still hadn't found a Guru.

There were days when I would come back from class feeling disappointed and end up crying. I questioned my progress, my decisions and whether I was ever going to find my place in this art form.

I remember feeling that I was constantly chasing Kathak, but somehow the art wasn't choosing me back.

It was an emotionally confusing phase. But somehow, I kept going.

During this time, I also attended workshops by Pt. Rajendra Gangani ji and Vidushi Shama Bhate ji. Every opportunity to learn added something new to my understanding of Kathak.

Those two and a half years passed rather quickly. Eventually, I had to leave my job, the pandemic happened, and I returned home.

## The search continued, this time online

During one of the workshops, I had the opportunity to meet Guru Smt. Swati Sinha ji. I was deeply drawn to her teaching and wanted to learn from her. However, she wasn't offering online classes at that time, and attending in person wasn't an immediate possibility.

Meanwhile, I began learning from Smt. Radhika Sathe ji and Smt. Ishani Sathe ji, whom I had also met during a workshop and greatly admired. I learnt from them both online and offline. They helped me prepare for my Kathak examinations and continued to guide me even afterwards.

Then, one day, I came across a poster announcing that Swati Sinha ji was accepting students for online classes.

I joined, and this time, I didn't look back.

## And the journey continues...

Learning under Swati ji gave my Kathak journey a new direction. I got opportunities to travel and learn from her in person, and eventually, I also had the opportunity to perform with her troupe.

Life continued to bring its own turns. I got married and moved to Tallinn, Estonia. My surroundings changed once again, but this time, my connection with my Guru remained.

Looking back, I sometimes wonder whether I was really the one choosing Kathak all along. Perhaps it was Kathak that kept choosing me — finding its way back into my life whenever I thought I had moved away from it.

Today, I feel grateful for every teacher who has guided me, every student who has learnt from me, every fellow artist who has shared a stage or an idea, and every opportunity that has come my way. Even the pauses, uncertainties and difficult moments have shaped the journey in their own way.

I know there is still so much to learn, understand and explore. And I hope to keep doing just that — one class, one performance, one new discovery at a time.

The little girl who once stood stubbornly in a corner of a dance class has certainly come a long way.

And she still has a feeling that Kathak isn't done with her yet. ❤️
